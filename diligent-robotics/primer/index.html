Section 01

Who Diligent is

Diligent Robotics builds and operates Moxi — an autonomous mobile manipulation robot that runs clinical logistics inside working hospitals. Moxi fetches and delivers medications, lab samples, supplies and equipment so that nurses and pharmacy techs stop walking miles per shift. It is not a research platform and not a pilot: it runs revenue-generating routes in live U.S. health systems today.

Business model, and who actually signs

Robots-as-a-service, sold per hospital facility. Diligent has publicly indicated annual sales at each hospital expected to range between $200k and $400k. That figure is why per-site economics matter more here than raw scale does: this is a volume-of-sites business, not a handful of megadeals.

Who buys Moxi: health-system operations and supply-chain leadership, pharmacy directors, and nursing leadership — they buy labor hours back. Who reviews it: hospital IT, security and biomed, who own network access, data handling and anything touching patient-adjacent systems. Where this conversation sits: with Diligent's own engineering and infrastructure leadership — training compute and fleet data — not with their hospital customers. We are not in their sales path.

As of September 2025 distribution also runs through Swisslog Healthcare (and its TransLogic pneumatic-tube business), which resells Moxi as a turnkey last-mile offering into Swisslog's installed base. Read that as a step-change in site count, which is the same as a step-change in fleet data volume.

Sources: diligentrobots.com company pages and press releases (captured 2026-08-14); Moxi 2.0 launch release, Austin, October 28, 2025.

Section 04

The layer deeper — what Latitude + Megaport actually change for Diligent

A generic infrastructure pitch is not useful here. This is the version specific to Diligent's actual constraints.

The one-sentence thesis. Diligent has built a data flywheel — ~100 robots generating fleet data that trains a foundation model that makes the robots better — and every turn of that flywheel gets more expensive on metered cloud, because it is simultaneously an egress problem, a burst GPU problem, and a data-residency problem.

Latitude sells the burst GPU as dedicated bare metal at a fixed price. Megaport moves the data there over private circuits instead of the public internet. Same company, one bill.

Their four real constraints, and the specific answer to each

Diligent's constraintWhy it bitesWhat we change
Fleet telemetry volume off hospital floors. A dataset "heading to petabytes" has to physically move from 25+ facilities to wherever training happens. On hyperscalers the ingest is cheap and the egress is not. Every time they move that corpus — to retrain, to re-label, to migrate — they pay again. Petabyte-scale egress is a five-to-six-figure line item that grows with site count. Megaport private circuits (Port + VXC) from each hospital region into Latitude. Private fabric, no public-internet backhaul, predictable port-based pricing rather than per-GB metering. The published Latitude customer proof point is Shinami: 79% compute-cost reduction and egress cost to zero. Source: latitude.sh/customers/shinami — https://www.latitude.sh/customers/shinami, HTTP 200, retrieved 2026-08-14. The page's own stat cards read verbatim "79% reduction in compute costs" and "100% reduction in egress costs"; the narrative gives the underlying numbers as a validator bill of ~$4,000/mo → ~$850/mo and egress of $300–500/mo → essentially zero, absorbed by Latitude's 20 TB/month per-server allowance.
Burst training and simulation compute for navigation and manipulation — including Omniverse simulation, which is GPU-heavy and spiky by nature. Training is bursty; inference on the robot is not. Reserved hyperscaler capacity sized for the burst idles between runs. On-demand sized for the average makes every training cycle slow. Latitude dedicated GPU bare metal — NVIDIA H100, A100, L40S on AMD EPYC hosts, provisioned in minutes across 24+ locations. No hypervisor tax, no noisy neighbours, a fixed monthly number instead of a variable one. Sized for the burst, and the burst is the whole job.
Clinical data-residency and HIPAA-adjacent handling. They are SOC 2 Type 2 and sell into hospital IT and security review. Robot logs from a hospital floor are patient-adjacent even when they are not PHI: video and audio of care areas, timestamps, room-level movement. Whatever path that data takes, a hospital security review will ask about it — and "it traverses the public internet" is a hard sentence to defend in that room. Named single-tenant hardware in a named facility, reached over a private circuit. That is a materially easier diagram to put in front of a hospital security committee than shared multi-tenant compute — and it is the same diagram at every site.
Site count is about to jump via the Swisslog channel. Per-site infrastructure cost that is comfortable at 25 facilities is worth re-checking at 100+, against $200k–$400k of expected annual revenue per hospital. Port-based fabric economics scale with sites, not with bytes — so per-site cost stops tracking how chatty the robots get.

Proposed architecture — concrete enough to whiteboard

  1. Edge (unchanged, theirs): Moxi 2.0 on NVIDIA Jetson, running inference on-robot. We do not touch the robot — worth being explicit about up front.
  2. Aggregation: per-facility or per-region collection point, feeding a Megaport Port; multi-site regions consolidate through an MCR so new hospitals attach to existing fabric instead of triggering new physical builds.
  3. Transport: Megaport VXCs from those ports into the Latitude region holding the training cluster — private, no public-internet hop, and VXCs to AWS as a private on-ramp so anything that must stay on the incumbent still moves privately.
  4. Storage: VAST for the hot training corpus that the GPUs actually read; Wasabi for the cold multi-petabyte archive of historical deliveries — with no egress fee when a future model wants to re-read the whole history. That last point is the one that matters for a flywheel.
  5. Compute: Latitude bare metal GPU (H100 / A100 / L40S) for foundation-model training and Omniverse simulation. Fixed monthly cost, dedicated, deploy in minutes.
  6. Return path: trained model artifacts ship back out to the fleet over the same private fabric.

The metric to put a number against

Lead with cost per effective GPU-hour of training, and its partner, total cost to move one petabyte of fleet data into a training run. Those two are where the difference is structural rather than promotional: dedicated bare metal removes the virtualization overhead and the idle-reservation waste, and private fabric removes per-GB egress from the equation entirely.

The business framing that lands with a CEO rather than an infra lead: "your cost per training cycle should stop scaling with your fleet size." For a company whose product improves as a direct function of how much fleet data it can afford to train on, that is a product-velocity argument, not an IT-savings argument.

Honesty guardrail. Shinami's 79% / zero-egress result is a published Latitude customer outcome (https://www.latitude.sh/customers/shinami, retrieved 2026-08-14), not a forecast for Diligent. It is cited as evidence that the model works, and it is cited with the URL so it can be checked. Two caveats stated up front: it is a Web3 validator workload, not a robotics-telemetry one, and the zero egress is Latitude's included 20 TB/month per server doing the work, not an unmetered promise. Nobody should say "you will save 79%" — we have not seen Diligent's bill, and we will not pretend otherwise.

Section 05

Five questions we want answered

These are the genuine unknowns from Section 03. The note under each one says why we are asking, so the reasoning is on the table rather than behind it.

1. "Moxi 2.0 carries 10x the compute of 1.0 — how much of that is running inference on-robot versus feeding data back for the next foundation-model train?"

Establishes the edge/cloud split, which determines whether anything we do is relevant at all. If it is overwhelmingly on-robot, we should know that in the first ten minutes.

2. "You've said the deployment dataset is heading to petabytes. What does it cost you today to move one full training corpus from the hospital sites into wherever you train — and does that number change when Swisslog adds sites?"

The egress question in concrete terms. Site growth through the Swisslog channel is the variable most likely to change the answer, which is why the two halves are asked together.

3. "When you retrain the foundation model, are you re-reading the full historical corpus or a sampled slice — and is that a technical choice or a cost choice?"

The question we most want answered. If the constraint is cost rather than method, then a no-egress archive changes what is affordable to train on — a model-quality outcome rather than an IT one. If it is a deliberate technical choice, that is a different conversation and a shorter one.

4. "How much of your GPU spend is burst training and Omniverse simulation versus steady-state, and what happens to the reserved capacity between training runs?"

If reserved capacity idles between runs, dedicated bare metal wins on arithmetic rather than on preference. If it does not, the arithmetic does not favour us and we should find that out early.

5. "When a hospital's security review asks where robot data physically goes and what network it crosses — what's the current answer, and how long does that review take?"

Private fabric may be worth more as sales-cycle acceleration than as a bandwidth saving. For a company selling into 25+ health systems and scaling through a channel, a shorter security review is the bigger number.

Section 06

Fair questions, and our answers

Four reasonable challenges to this proposal, and the honest response to each. Where the answer depends on something we do not know, it says so.

1. "We're already on AWS — and we have AWS credits from the Physical AI Fellowship."

Our answer: "Then don't move inference — the credits are the right tool for the workload they cover. The case is for putting burst training and the petabyte archive on dedicated hardware, with a Megaport VXC as a private on-ramp back to AWS so it stays one fabric rather than two clouds." The distinction that matters: credits are finite and dated; a petabyte-scale egress line is neither.

2. "Bare metal means we lose elasticity."

Our answer: "Latitude provisions in minutes, not weeks, across 24+ locations. Training bursts are scheduled rather than unpredictable, so the elasticity that actually matters here is provisioning speed rather than per-second billing. If reserved capacity is sitting idle between runs today, that gap is the number worth putting on the table — and if it isn't, this argument doesn't apply."

3. "Hospital data can't leave the building / our security review will kill this."

Our answer: "That's the argument for this. To whatever extent any part of that path runs over the public internet today, this replaces it with a private circuit to named single-tenant hardware in a named facility — an easier diagram for a security committee, and the same diagram at every site you add."

4. "We're a robotics company, not an infrastructure company — we don't want to run this."

Our answer: "Agreed, and you shouldn't have to. It's one vendor, one bill and one support desk — Latitude is Megaport. You keep the flywheel; we keep the transit and the tin."
